> Total: 60 marks. Take g = 9.81 m/s², G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m, μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). Neglect air resistance unless stated.

### A-1 | Model Set A | 3
A ball is projected from level ground with a speed of 20 m/s and lands 30 m away. Find the two possible angles of projection. Which of the two gives the greater maximum height?

#### Answer
:::final
θ ≈ 23.7° and θ ≈ 66.3°; the 66.3° projection rises higher (≈ 17.1 m against ≈ 3.3 m).
:::

#### Explanation
For equal launch and landing heights, R = u² sin2θ / g, so two complementary angles give the same range.

:::eq
sin2θ = Rg/u² = 30(9.81)/20² = 0.7358
2θ = 47.4° or 132.6° ⟹ θ = 23.7° or 66.3°
H = u² sin²θ / (2g): H₁ ≈ 3.3 m, H₂ ≈ 17.1 m
:::

The steeper projection has the larger vertical component, so it rises higher (and stays in the air longer).

### A-2 | Model Set A | 4
A stone is thrown horizontally at 12 m/s from the top of a 45 m cliff over level ground. Find (a) the time of flight, (b) the horizontal distance from the foot of the cliff to the landing point, and (c) the speed and direction of the stone just before impact.

#### Answer
:::final
(a) 3.03 s  (b) 36.3 m  (c) 32.0 m/s at 68.0° below the horizontal
:::

#### Explanation
The horizontal velocity stays constant; the vertical motion is free fall from rest.

:::eq
t = √(2h/g) = √(2 × 45 / 9.81) = 3.03 s
x = vₓt = 12 × 3.03 = 36.3 m
v_y = gt = 29.7 m/s
v = √(12² + 29.7²) = 32.0 m/s
tan φ = 29.7/12 ⟹ φ = 68.0° below the horizontal
:::

### A-3 | Model Set A | 2
A 70 kg person stands on a weighing scale in a lift. Find the scale reading (the normal force) when the lift (a) accelerates upward at 1.5 m/s², and (b) accelerates downward at 2.0 m/s².

#### Answer
:::final
(a) ≈ 792 N  (b) ≈ 547 N
:::

#### Explanation
The scale reads the normal force N, not the gravitational force. Take upward as positive: N − mg = ma.

:::eq

(b) N = m(g − a) = 70(9.81 − 2.0) = 546.7 N

### A-4 | Model Set A | 2
Assume the period T of a simple pendulum depends only on its mass m, its length l and the gravitational acceleration g. Use dimensional analysis to find how T depends on these quantities.

#### Answer
:::final
T ∝ √(l/g); T does not depend on m.
:::

#### Explanation
Write T = k mᵃ lᵇ gᶜ and equate the powers of M, L and T on both sides.

:::eq
[T] = Mᵃ Lᵇ (LT⁻²)ᶜ = Mᵃ Lᵇ⁺ᶜ T⁻²ᶜ
M: a = 0
T: −2c = 1 ⟹ c = −1/2
L: b + c = 0 ⟹ b = 1/2
T = k √(l/g)
:::

Dimensional analysis cannot fix the dimensionless constant k (its value is 2π).

### A-5 | Model Set A | 2
A 1200 kg car travelling at 15 m/s is brought to rest by a constant braking force over a distance of 30 m. Find the magnitude of the braking force and the time taken to stop.

#### Answer
:::final
F = 4500 N; t = 4.0 s
:::

#### Explanation
By the work-energy theorem, the work done by the brakes equals the loss of kinetic energy.

:::eq
F d = ½mv² = ½(1200)(15)² = 1.35 × 10⁵ J
F = 1.35 × 10⁵ / 30 = 4500 N
Average speed = 15/2 = 7.5 m/s ⟹ t = 30/7.5 = 4.0 s
:::

### A-6 | Model Set A | 3

### A-7 | Model Set A | 4

#### Answer
:::final
V ≈ 1.33 m/s in the original direction of the 2.0 kg block; kinetic energy lost ≈ 32.7 J

#### Explanation
Momentum is conserved. Take the direction of the 2.0 kg block as positive.

:::eq

### A-15 | Model Set A | 2
Why does the amplitude of a real forced oscillator remain finite at resonance? State the effect of increasing the damping on the resonance peak.

#### Answer
:::final
Damping removes energy; the amplitude stops growing when the power supplied by the driving force equals the power dissipated. More damping lowers and broadens the peak.
:::

#### Explanation
Near resonance the driver feeds energy into the oscillator in step with its motion. Dissipation grows with amplitude (for viscous damping, the power lost is proportional to the square of the speed), so a steady state is reached at a finite amplitude.

Increasing the damping lowers the peak amplitude, broadens the resonance curve and shifts the peak to a slightly lower frequency than the natural frequency.

### A-16 | Model Set A | 3
Taking air as an ideal gas with molar mass 0.029 kg/mol and γ = 1.4, find the speed of sound at 300 K (a) using Laplace's adiabatic formula, and (b) using Newton's isothermal formula. (c) By what percentage does Newton's value fall short of Laplace's?

#### Answer

(a) ≈ 347 m/s  (b) ≈ 293 m/s  (c) ≈ 15.5%
:::

(b) v = √(8.314 × 300 / 0.029) = 293 m/s

The ratio of the two values is √γ = 1.18.

### B-2 | Model Set B | 3
A stone is thrown from the top of a 50 m cliff with speed 15 m/s at 30° above the horizontal. Find (a) the time to reach the ground at the foot of the cliff, (b) the horizontal distance from the foot of the cliff to the landing point, and (c) the impact speed (use energy conservation).

:::final
(a) 4.05 s  (b) 52.6 m  (c) 34.7 m/s

(a) Find the period of a simple pendulum of length 1.5 m for small oscillations. (b) What length gives a period of exactly 2.00 s? (c) Does the period depend on the mass of the bob? Explain briefly.

### B-21 | Model Set B | 2
Explain, using band theory, why the electrical resistivity of a metal increases with temperature while that of an intrinsic semiconductor decreases.

#### Answer
:::final
In a metal the number of free carriers is nearly fixed, and more lattice vibrations scatter them more strongly. In a semiconductor, heating excites many more carriers across the energy gap, and this outweighs the extra scattering.
:::

#### Explanation
A metal has a partly filled band (or overlapping bands), so conduction electrons are already plentiful. A rise in temperature increases lattice vibrations, which scatter the electrons more often, reducing their mobility and increasing the resistivity.

In an intrinsic semiconductor the valence band is full and the conduction band is empty at low temperature, separated by a small gap. The number of carriers excited across the gap increases rapidly with temperature, so the conductivity rises and the resistivity falls, even though scattering also increases.
