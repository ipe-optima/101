### A-1 | Model Set A | 2
State the number of orbitals and the maximum number of electrons in the shell with n = 3. List the subshells present and the number of orbitals in each.

#### Answer
:::final
9 orbitals and 18 electrons
:::

Subshells: **3s** (1 orbital), **3p** (3 orbitals), **3d** (5 orbitals).

#### Explanation
For a given n, l = 0, 1, … n−1, and each subshell contains (2l + 1) orbitals.

:::eq
n = 3 ⟹ l = 0, 1, 2 ⟹ 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 orbitals (= n²)
Maximum electrons = 2n² = 2(9) = 18
:::

Each orbital holds at most two electrons of opposite spin (Pauli exclusion principle).

### A-4 | Model Set A | 3
Calculate the root-mean-square speed of O₂ molecules at 27 °C. How does it compare with that of H₂ at the same temperature? (R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; M(O₂) = 32.0 g/mol; M(H₂) = 2.016 g/mol)

#### Answer
:::final
v<sub>rms</sub>(O₂) ≈ 484 m/s  |  v<sub>rms</sub>(H₂) ≈ 1.93 × 10³ m/s (about 4 times faster)
:::

#### Explanation
:::eq
T = 27 + 273 = 300 K,  M = 32.0 × 10⁻³ kg/mol
v<sub>rms</sub> = √(3RT/M) = √(3 × 8.314 × 300 / 0.0320)

:::eq

v(H₂) = 3.98 × 484 ≈ 1.93 × 10³ m/s
:::

Lighter molecules have the same average kinetic energy (3/2 kT) but move faster.

### A-6 | Model Set A | 4
(i) Predict the major product of the reaction of 2-methylpropene with HBr and justify your answer. (ii) Name the electrophile in the nitration of benzene and show how it is generated from HNO₃ and H₂SO₄.

#### Answer
(i) **(CH₃)₃CBr**, 2-bromo-2-methylpropane (*tert*-butyl bromide).

(ii) The electrophile is the **nitronium ion, NO₂⁺**; the product is nitrobenzene.

#### Explanation
##### (i) Markovnikov addition
:::eq
(CH₃)₂C=CH₂ + H⁺ → (CH₃)₃C⁺  →(Br⁻)→  (CH₃)₃CBr
:::

H⁺ adds to the CH₂ carbon (the one with more hydrogens), giving a **tertiary carbocation** stabilized by the +I effect and hyperconjugation of three methyl groups. The alternative primary cation (CH₃)₂CHCH₂⁺ is far less stable, so 1-bromo-2-methylpropane is only a minor product.

##### (ii) Generation of NO₂⁺
:::eq
HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻
C₆H₆ + NO₂⁺ → [C₆H₆NO₂]⁺ (arenium ion) → C₆H₅NO₂ + H⁺
:::

H₂SO₄, the stronger acid, protonates HNO₃, which then loses water to give NO₂⁺. The arenium ion loses H⁺ to restore the aromatic ring.

### A-8 | Model Set A | 3
Calculate the wavelength of the photon emitted when the electron in a hydrogen atom falls from n = 4 to n = 2. Name the series and the region of the spectrum. (R<sub>H</sub> = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹)

### A-10 | Model Set A | 2
The first ionization energy of nitrogen (1402 kJ/mol) is higher than that of oxygen (1314 kJ/mol), although oxygen lies to its right in the same period. Explain.

#### Answer
Nitrogen's half-filled 2p³ configuration is especially stable, whereas oxygen's 2p⁴ has a paired electron that is easier to remove.

#### Explanation
- **N (2p³):** the three 2p electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins, which gives extra exchange stabilization and no pairing repulsion. Removing one electron breaks this stable arrangement.
- **O (2p⁴):** one 2p orbital holds a pair of electrons. The electron–electron repulsion within the pair raises its energy, so it is removed more easily; the resulting O⁺ has the stable half-filled 2p³ configuration.

### A-11 | Model Set A | 3
A buffer contains 0.150 M NH₃ and 0.300 M NH₄Cl. Calculate its pH (K<sub>b</sub> of NH₃ = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, pK<sub>b</sub> ≈ 4.74). Which component neutralizes a small amount of added strong acid?

#### Answer
:::final
pH ≈ 8.96  |  The base component, NH₃, neutralizes added H⁺.
:::

#### Explanation
:::eq
pOH = pK<sub>b</sub> + log([NH₄⁺]/[NH₃]) = 4.74 + log(0.300/0.150)
= 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04
pH = 14 − 5.04 = 8.96
:::

Cross-check using the conjugate acid (pK<sub>a</sub> of NH₄⁺ = 14 − 4.74 = 9.26): pH = 9.26 + log(0.150/0.300) = 9.26 − 0.30 = 8.96 ✓.

Added H⁺ reacts with the weak base: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺. Added OH⁻ would be consumed by NH₄⁺. The ratio [NH₄⁺]/[NH₃] changes only slightly, so the pH barely moves.

### A-12 | Model Set A | 4
The rate constant of a reaction doubles when the temperature is raised from 300 K to 310 K. (a) Calculate the activation energy (R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹). (b) How does a catalyst increase the rate at constant temperature?

#### Answer
:::final
(a) E<sub>a</sub> ≈ 53.6 kJ/mol
:::

(b) A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a **lower activation energy**, so a larger fraction of collisions are effective and k increases; the catalyst is not consumed.

#### Explanation
:::eq
ln(k₂/k₁) = (E<sub>a</sub>/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
ln 2 = (E<sub>a</sub>/8.314)(1/300 − 1/310)
0.693 = (E<sub>a</sub>/8.314)(1.075 × 10⁻⁴)
E<sub>a</sub> = (0.693 × 8.314)/(1.075 × 10⁻⁴) = 5.36 × 10⁴ J/mol
:::

From k = A e<sup>−Ea/RT</sup>, lowering E<sub>a</sub> raises k exponentially, while the overall ΔH of the reaction is unchanged.

### A-13 | Model Set A | 3
State the hybridization of the central atom in NH₃ and H₂O and describe their shapes. Why are the bond angles (107° and 104.5°) smaller than the ideal tetrahedral angle of 109.5°?

#### Answer
Both N and O are **sp³** hybridized. NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal; H₂O is bent (V-shaped). Lone pairs repel more strongly than bond pairs, which compresses the bond angles.

| Molecule | Bond pairs | Lone pairs | Shape | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CH₄ | 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| NH₃ | 3 | 1 | Trigonal pyramidal | 107° |
| H₂O | 2 | 2 | Bent | 104.5° |

#### Explanation
In each molecule the four electron pairs point toward the corners of a tetrahedron. A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it occupies more space than a bonding pair. The repulsion order is

:::eq
lone pair–lone pair &gt; lone pair–bond pair &gt; bond pair–bond pair
:::

H₂O has two lone pairs, so it shows the largest compression of the H–O–H angle; NH₃ has one, so its compression is smaller.

### A-14 | Model Set A | 3
Use Slater's rules to calculate the effective nuclear charge experienced by a 4s electron in a potassium atom (Z = 19). What does the result suggest about the ease of removing this electron?

#### Answer
:::final
Z<sub>eff</sub> = 19 − 16.8 = 2.2
:::

#### Explanation
Potassium: 1s² 2s²2p⁶ 3s²3p⁶ 4s¹. Slater groups: (1s)(2s,2p)(3s,3p)(3d)(4s,4p).

| Electrons | Number | Shielding each | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same group (4s, 4p), other than the one considered | 0 | 0.35 | 0 |
| n−1 shell (3s, 3p) | 8 | 0.85 | 6.80 |
| n−2 and lower (1s, 2s, 2p) | 10 | 1.00 | 10.00 |

:::eq
σ = 0 + 6.80 + 10.00 = 16.80
Z<sub>eff</sub> = Z − σ = 19 − 16.80 = 2.2
:::

The outer electron feels only a small net attraction, so it is held loosely. This is why potassium has a low ionization energy and readily forms K⁺.

### A-15 | Model Set A | 3
Write the van der Waals equation for n moles of a real gas and explain the meaning of the constants *a* and *b*. Which gas, N₂ or NH₃, has the larger value of *a*, and why?

#### Answer
:::eq
(P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT
:::

**NH₃** has the larger *a* because of strong intermolecular attraction (hydrogen bonding).

#### Explanation
- **a** corrects for intermolecular **attractive forces**, which reduce the pressure the gas exerts on the walls. The term an²/V² is added back to P.
- **b** corrects for the finite **volume of the molecules** (excluded volume per mole), so the free volume is V − nb.
- Approximate values: *a* ≈ 1.4 L² atm mol⁻² for N₂ and ≈ 4.2 L² atm mol⁻² for NH₃. Hydrogen-bonded, polar NH₃ molecules attract each other far more strongly than nonpolar N₂ molecules.

### A-16 | Model Set A | 4
(a) Arrange HOCl, HClO₂, HClO₃ and HClO₄ in order of increasing acid strength and justify your order. (b) Why is HI a much stronger acid than HF, although fluorine is more electronegative than iodine?

#### Answer
:::final
(a) HOCl &lt; HClO₂ &lt; HClO₃ &lt; HClO₄
:::

(b) The H–I bond is far weaker than the H–F bond, so HI ionizes much more readily.

#### Explanation
##### (a) Chlorine oxyacids
| Acid | Oxidation state of Cl | Terminal (non-OH) O | Approx. pK<sub>a</sub> |
|---|---|---|---|
| HOCl | +1 | 0 | ≈ 7.5 |
| HClO₂ | +3 | 1 | ≈ 2 |
| HClO₃ | +5 | 2 | ≈ −1 |
| HClO₄ | +7 | 3 | ≈ −8 |

Each extra terminal oxygen (and higher oxidation state of Cl) pulls electron density away from the O–H bond and delocalizes the negative charge of the conjugate base over more oxygens, so the anion is more stable and the acid stronger. (pK<sub>a</sub> values are approximate.)

##### (b) HI vs HF
Bond enthalpy of H–F ≈ 565 kJ/mol, whereas H–I ≈ 299 kJ/mol. Down the group, the H–X bond becomes longer and weaker, so acid strength rises: HF &lt; HCl &lt; HBr &lt; HI. Bond strength outweighs electronegativity here.

Distinguish between the molecularity and the order of a reaction.

#### Answer
| | Molecularity | Order |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Number of species colliding in a single elementary step | Sum of the exponents of concentrations in the experimental rate law |

### A-19 | Model Set A | 2
Both CO₂ and SO₂ contain polar bonds, yet CO₂ is nonpolar while SO₂ is polar. Explain.

#### Answer
CO₂ is linear and symmetric, so its bond dipoles cancel; SO₂ is bent, so its bond dipoles do not cancel.

#### Explanation
- **CO₂ (O=C=O, 180°):** the two C=O dipoles are equal and point in opposite directions, so the net dipole moment is zero.
- **SO₂ (bent, ≈ 119°):** sulfur carries a lone pair, so the molecule is V-shaped. The two S=O dipoles add up to a net dipole (μ ≈ 1.6 D), making SO₂ polar.

### A-21 | Model Set A | 2
What is meant by knocking in a petrol engine? Name two refinery processes used to raise the octane number of gasoline.

#### Answer
**Knocking** is the premature auto-ignition of part of the fuel–air mixture before the flame front reaches it. Octane number can be raised by **catalytic reforming**, **isomerization** or **alkylation**.

#### Explanation
- Knocking produces a metallic "pinging" sound, loss of power and, over time, engine damage.
- Straight-chain alkanes are prone to knocking; the refinery processes above convert them to branched alkanes and aromatics, which burn smoothly and have higher octane numbers.

Write the ground-state electronic configurations of Fe (Z = 26) and Fe³⁺. Why is Fe³⁺ particularly stable?

#### Answer
:::final
Fe: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶  |  Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵
:::

#### Explanation

### B-4 | Model Set B | 2
BF₃ is a planar molecule whereas NF₃ is pyramidal. Explain the difference in terms of hybridization.

#### Answer
Boron in BF₃ is **sp²** hybridized with no lone pair (trigonal planar); nitrogen in NF₃ is **sp³** hybridized with one lone pair (trigonal pyramidal).

#### Explanation
- **BF₃:** boron has three valence electrons, forming three σ bonds with no lone pair. Three sp² orbitals lie in one plane at 120°.

### B-6 | Model Set B | 4
(a) Calculate the pH of 0.100 M acetic acid (K<sub>a</sub> = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵). (b) Find its percentage ionization. (c) What happens to the percentage ionization when the solution is diluted to 0.0100 M? Support your answer with a calculation.

### B-7 | Model Set B | 3

### B-9 | Model Set B | 3

### B-10 | Model Set B | 3

### B-13 | Model Set B | 4
(a) Derive the integrated rate law for a first-order reaction A → products and show that its half-life is independent of the initial concentration. (b) A first-order reaction has k = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹. How long does it take for 75 % of the reactant to decompose?

#### Answer
:::final
(a) ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt; t<sub>½</sub> = 0.693/k  |  (b) t ≈ 2.77 × 10³ s (≈ 46 min)
:::

#### Explanation
##### (a) Derivation
:::eq
−d[A]/dt = k[A]  ⟹  d[A]/[A] = −k dt
∫ d[A]/[A] from [A]₀ to [A] = −k ∫ dt from 0 to t
ln([A]/[A]₀) = −kt  ⟹  ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt
:::

At t = t<sub>½</sub>, [A] = [A]₀/2:

:::eq
ln(1/2) = −k t<sub>½</sub>  ⟹  t<sub>½</sub> = ln 2 / k = 0.693 / k
:::

[A]₀ cancels out, so the half-life does not depend on the initial concentration. A plot of ln[A] against t is a straight line of slope −k.

##### (b) Time for 75 % decomposition
:::eq
[A]/[A]₀ = 25/100
t = (1/k) ln([A]₀/[A]) = (1/5.00 × 10⁻⁴) × ln 4 = 2000 × 1.386 = 2.77 × 10³ s
:::

This equals two half-lives (t<sub>½</sub> = 1386 s), as expected for 75 % conversion.

### B-14 | Model Set B | 2
Chlorine has a more negative electron affinity than fluorine, although fluorine is more electronegative. Explain.

#### Answer
Fluorine's 2p subshell is so small and compact that an incoming electron is strongly repelled by the electrons already present; chlorine's larger 3p subshell has more room.

#### Explanation
- **F (2p⁵):** high electron density in a small volume gives strong electron–electron repulsion, which offsets part of the attraction from the nucleus. Energy released ≈ 328 kJ/mol.
- **Cl (3p⁵):** the 3p subshell is larger, so repulsion is much less and more energy is released ≈ 349 kJ/mol.

Electronegativity concerns an atom in a bond and keeps rising toward F, whereas electron affinity is an isolated-atom energy that is affected by this repulsion.

#### Answer
:::eq

:::

**Z less than 1:** attractive forces dominate. **Z greater than 1:** repulsion (finite molecular size) dominates.

### B-21 | Model Set B | 2
Define biochemical oxygen demand (BOD). Why does BOD rise sharply in a lake that receives fertilizer runoff?

#### Answer
**BOD** is the amount of dissolved oxygen consumed by aerobic microorganisms in decomposing the organic matter in a water sample over a fixed period (usually 5 days at 20 °C), expressed in mg O₂ per litre.

#### Explanation
